# The Whole Truth: what its home page says, and what we take (18-10-2026)

Read from the owner's screen recording of thewholetruthfoods.com's home page on a desktop (86 s, the whole page top to footer, the story overlay opened). Patterns only, never its look (F14, F16). Adopting any of it is UX7's work, with the owner's word.

## What it wants to say
One claim, proved section by section: **"We're re-building the world's trust in food."** Trust is earned by disclosure, so every block shows something a food brand usually hides. Read the headings alone and the story is whole:

1. We're re-building the world's trust in food. (the promise)
2. Short story looong of why we exist. (why: brands hide what is in their food)
3. We make real food. (the products, every ingredient declared)
4. Real food is flawed. (the confession: it does not always look perfect)
5. We're also fixing food journalism. (teaching: a newsletter, an academy, an ingredient dictionary)
6. Real food made by real people. (the factory in 3D, "FSSC certified")
7. Real people. Real love. Stars ♥ us! Stop the press! (proof by others)
8. Come, join the fam! (the ask)

## Its design language, and how well it communicates
- **Three type voices, each with one job.** A heavy condensed sans for claims (two short lines, flat statements); a monospace, like a typed label, for the plain facts under them; a handwriting in the brand's berry for asides ("And those flaws are perfect!", "So is Barry!", "(and we didn't even have to pay for it)"). The claim, the evidence and the human aside never share a voice, so a glance tells which is which. Works well.
- **One accent colour that means "this is the point".** Near-black on white; the berry marks only what matters: the underline under "trust in food", the outlined button, the asides, the overlap in the diagram. Product packs carry their own colours, kept inside the cards. Works well, until one hot-magenta promotion banner breaks the calm (the sales layer, not the story).
- **Diagrams that argue one thing.** A Venn of "What you eat" and "What you know", the overlap filled, an arrow: "We're working on closing the gap". One shape, one fill, one sentence. The strongest block on the page.
- **Underlines as emphasis.** In the body, the promise is underlined ("we declare each and every ingredient, proudly, upfront"), a reader's pencil rather than bold.
- **Confession as a trust signal.** "Real food is flawed" and the story's "ugh!" admit limits before a sceptic finds them.
- **A tool on the home page.** ChemX: "Found an ingredient you can't pronounce? Allow us to help", with a search field, inside a card.
- **Show the making.** The factory in 3D, tabs per product, "PS: Our Bada Barkhana is an FSSC certified facility :)".
- **A fact strip, not a slogan strip.** The black bar on top: "Discounts auto-applied in cart · Orders dispatched within 48-72 hours · Earn 5% loyalty points on orders above 3,000".
- **Where it weakens:** small monospace and handwriting are hard to read at body size; five carousels and two marquees hide content behind arrows and add motion that says nothing; celebrity rows and press logos are bulk by our principle 10; the long scroll delays the shop.

## What we borrow (it fits our language)
| Theirs | Ours |
|---|---|
| Headings that are flat claims, readable alone as the story | Each result and section heading a sentence that is the answer: "Meets the default benchmarks", "Short of the lowest-year DSCR by Rs. 1,20,000" (D-UX-99's bars already do it) |
| Three voices, one job each | Our sans for the claim, tabular figures for the engine's facts and "how it is worked" (our ingredient list), and the aside in a margin note (below), with no new font |
| One accent that means "the point" | One colour for the benchmark line and what crosses it; nothing else in it (`docs/DESIGN-MATHS.md` decides the share) |
| The Venn that closes a gap | Our gap is the shortfall to a benchmark, and Make it viable closes it: one bar, the benchmark line, the gap filled, the lever named |
| Confession first | Provisional said as loudly as final; the assumptions and what is missing on the front, never in a footnote |
| Show the factory | Show the engine: every rule with its source and date (`docs/RULES.md`), worked twice |
| A fact strip | "Free · No sign-up · Your figures stay in your browser", facts we can keep |
| A tool on the home page | One door per tool already; a one-figure check on home only if it keeps D-UX-12's ceiling |

## What we leave
Handwriting as a font (weight, and a lender reading a printout needs sober type: documents stay formal), carousels and marquees (a borrower should see everything at once), celebrity and testimonial rows (none exist yet, and never invented), the hot promotion banner, cheek in anything a lender reads.

## Where we are our own (bespoke)
- **The auditor's tick mark** in place of their doodles: a small drawn tick and a pencil note in the margin beside a figure ("worked twice, agrees to the paisa"; "your benchmark, default 1.25"). It is the finance trade's own annotation, it says something, and it reuses home's drawn line.
- **The benchmark line** as the site's one recurring shape, the way their Venn is theirs: every ratio a bar against one rule, on every tool and in the documents.
- **Provisional is dashed, final is solid**: a result's outline tells its state before its words do.
- **Indian figures set as a ledger**: lakh and crore grouping in tabular figures, right-aligned, the rupee once per column.
- **The tone**: plain and warm on the pages ("what you can change"), formal in the documents; candid about limits, never cheeky about money.

## Tradeoffs for the owner
1. Personality against a lender's trust: keep the voice on the web pages' headings and asides only, the documents formal (recommended).
2. A handwriting font costs weight on every page and reads badly small: drawn marks instead (recommended).
3. Carousels and motion look alive but hide content and break principle 10: none (recommended).
4. A story-scroll home sells the why but delays the tools: headings as the story, the tool doors kept above the fold (recommended).
5. Social proof: until real users speak, proof by method (worked twice, sources dated); never invented quotes (recommended).
