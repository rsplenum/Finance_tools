FinSight · Product and document architecture review

The project report needs a stronger argument, not more decoration.

Independent review of the uploaded “Project Report Redesign” proposal and its 15-page Meera Foods specimen. This is a product-level redesign brief: what to keep, what to change, what is missing, and the report architecture FinSight should build toward.

Reviewed: 15 November 2026Source: uploaded ZIP · index.html + 15 page imagesExternal benchmarks: UNIDO · World Bank · ADBAudience: borrower, banker, credit analyst
Overall verdict

Approve the direction. Do not approve the specimen as the final report standard.

The proposal improves the interface and introduces a proper narrative layer, but its specimen is still a financial summary with a thin business story. It has more exhibits than evidence. It needs a stronger project logic, a defensible market and operating case, traceable assumptions, and a hierarchy that makes each page answer one decision question.

01 · Diagnosis

Your criticism is correct. Here is the deeper problem.

The sample is clean and legible. The weakness is not primarily typography. It is the relationship between evidence, explanation and financial output. The report shows that the numbers reconcile; it does not yet show, with comparable depth, why the project should exist, how it will operate, where its customers will come from, and why its assumptions deserve belief.

01

Financially articulate

Project cost, means of finance, projected P&L, balance sheet, cash flow, DSCR and sensitivities are visible. This is the strongest part of the specimen and should remain the deterministic core.

02

Commercially under-evidenced

Products, market, sales and promoter sections mostly state a story. They do not yet show enough customer, competitor, price, channel, sourcing or capacity evidence to support the story.

03

Exhibit-led, not question-led

Several charts are introduced because a chart exists, not because a visual is the clearest proof. The reader must decode chart, table and prose that often repeat one another.

Important distinction: “Meat” does not mean adding pages, paragraphs or charts. It means adding decision-grade evidence: quantities, sources, comparisons, operating logic, assumptions, constraints and consequences. A 12-page report with that evidence is stronger than a 35-page report full of generic prose.
02 · Disposition

What I approve, reject, revise and defer

The proposal contains several sound product decisions. The problem is that some are presented as if they solve report quality, when they mainly solve interface, rendering or monetisation.

Approve

Promoter-authored narrative sections

Keep a proper narrative layer for promoter profile, project rationale, products, process, market, implementation and risks. AI can help structure and draft it, but the borrower must review and attest to the facts.

Approve

Deterministic figures and figure slots

Keep the calculation engine as the sole authority for financial values. Use typed data bindings rather than letting a language model invent or retype numbers. Add provenance, units, periods and rounding rules to each slot.

Approve

One visual language across screen and PDF

HTML/CSS as a shared presentation layer is a sensible direction. It can improve layout consistency, accessibility and iteration. The exact PDF rendering vendor is an implementation choice, not a product principle.

Approve

Risk register and scenario analysis

Keep them, but deepen them. Risks should connect to assumptions, triggers, owners and mitigation. Scenarios should show combined downside and cash consequences.

Revise

Two-thirds questions / one-third preview

Potentially useful for the builder, but it is not a report-quality solution. Test it on desktop and mobile. The question interface should progressively reveal relevant inputs, not make every project look like a long form.

Revise

Fifteen-page specimen

Keep it as a visual prototype, not as the universal document specification. Build a short project profile, standard bankable report and detailed DPR as distinct output depths.

Revise

Optional “The write-up” step

Rename it “Project narrative & evidence”. It should be a guided evidence workflow. Some minimum project facts are essential; optional photos cannot substitute for market, promoter or technical evidence.

Reject

Annual reports as the report blueprint

Do not use listed-company annual reports as the primary structure. Borrow their editorial discipline, not their purpose or chapter order.

Reject

“A shop window” as the only free preview concept

A sample page and teaser chart can support paid conversion, but the preview must still communicate what the user has created. Do not let monetisation dictate the report's information architecture.

Reject

Digit-scanning as the only AI guardrail

A digit checker is brittle. It can miss written numbers, units and implied numerical claims; it can also block legitimate dates or user-supplied facts. Use structured slots and provenance validation instead.

Defer

Server-side report generation and payment gate

Decide after a threat model, privacy design and cost test. Server rendering can protect paid document assembly only if sensitive report data and the paid artifact are not already exposed in client state or APIs.

Defer

Photos and browser storage architecture

Useful for project evidence, but first define upload consent, file limits, retention, deletion, malware handling, image compression and how photos are referenced in the final report.

Security correction: moving PDF rendering to a server does not automatically secure the report. If the browser already receives all detailed financial outputs, statement data or report JSON, a user can still inspect those responses. Entitlement checks, sensitive data access and paid artifact generation must be enforced server-side. Treat the “server print” idea as one part of a wider access-control design.
03 · What top-tier project studies teach us

Use feasibility-study logic, not annual-report imitation.

I reviewed authoritative project-preparation and appraisal references. They are not all direct templates for a small Indian MSME loan report, but together they establish the right content logic: market, inputs, site, engineering, organisation, implementation, finance, risk and evidence must connect.

UNIDO · Industrial feasibility study manual

Most relevant structural benchmark. Its contents cover project background, market analysis and marketing concept, raw materials and supplies, location/site/environment, engineering and technology, organisation and overheads, human resources, implementation planning and budgeting, and financial analysis.

Its key lesson is integration: market requirements shape the production programme; capacity shapes inputs and equipment; those shape costs; implementation timing shapes funding needs. It explicitly treats assumptions and alternatives as things to justify, not merely list.

Read UNIDO manual

World Bank · Project Appraisal Document approach

Best benchmark for decision architecture. A PAD joins strategic context, project description, objectives, components, implementation arrangements, monitoring, risk and appraisal analysis. The detailed material is moved into annexes rather than forcing every technical schedule into the main narrative.

FinSight should borrow the layered structure: decision summary first, coherent argument in the body, inspectable technical and financial schedules in annexes.

Read World Bank project-financing toolkit

ADB · Prefeasibility study contents

Best benchmark for a concise study. ADB's toolkit gives a typical prefeasibility report sequence: executive summary, location map, introduction/background, project description, implementation plan, sustainability, financial and economic analysis, stakeholder comments, key risks, conclusions and annexes. It describes a 12–20 page PFS, but that is a prefeasibility context—not a universal page limit for FinSight.

Read ADB toolkit

UNIDO · Current industrial-park feasibility framing

Best reminder of scope. UNIDO describes site-specific feasibility as combining market identification and demand forecasts, phased master planning, technical designs, cost-benefit analysis, social/environmental assessment, institutional mapping, infrastructure, financing and economic impact.

For smaller projects, scale the depth down—but do not erase the categories that are material to that project.

Benchmark interpretation: FinSight is not a World Bank PAD, UNIDO consultant feasibility study or independent TEV report. It should adopt their analytical discipline while clearly labelling its own output as a promoter-prepared project report supported by FinSight tools.

04 · Proposed report architecture

A modular report with a clear narrative spine.

The main body should read like a guided argument. Put the answer first, explain the basis, then move detailed schedules to annexes. Each project type activates only the modules that matter.

Recommended default: 16–24 pages for a standard bankable MSME project report, excluding detailed annexes. This is a design range, not a rule. A simple trading/service proposal may be shorter; a manufacturing or construction project may need a longer technical annex or a separate DPR.

Cover & document control

Project title, legal borrower/promoter, location, report type, date, version, confidentiality, preparer statement. No chart. No financial dashboard.

Evidence: verified identity, project location, version metadata.

Executive decision brief

One-page answer: what is proposed, total project cost, funding ask, promoter contribution, purpose, expected operating scale, readiness, 3–5 key strengths, 3–5 material risks and conditions still open.

Evidence: engine summary + verified project facts.

Project at a glance

Project configuration, products/services, capacity, location, implementation period, employment, key facilities and a compact “how it works” strip. Avoid repeating the full five-year forecast.

Evidence: project profile, capacity basis, site details.

Promoter & enterprise

Ownership/legal form, promoter experience relevant to this project, roles, existing business track record, financial contribution, management gaps and external support required.

Evidence: KYC/entity docs where appropriate, experience records, net-worth/contribution support.

Project rationale & objectives

What problem/opportunity is being addressed? Why this location, product, scale and timing? What alternatives were considered and why was this configuration selected?

Evidence: promoter rationale, local context, alternatives and selection logic.

Products, customers & value proposition

Product/service specifications, grades/SKUs, use cases, target segments, quality standards, differentiation and expected customer value. Avoid generic “high demand” claims.

Evidence: product catalogue, samples/specifications, customer conversations, orders or enquiries.

Market & competition

Define geography and market boundary. Segment demand, identify buyer types, competitors/substitutes, price bands, distribution channels, seasonality, procurement behaviour and barriers to entry.

Evidence: dated sources, market survey, competitor observations, quotations, customer pipeline.

Sales & revenue build-up

Show how forecast sales are built from units × price × ramp-up, by product/customer/channel. Explain utilisation ramp, conversion assumptions, capacity constraints, credit terms and concentration.

Evidence: engine-linked volume/price drivers + order book or reasoned market assumptions.

Technical & operating plan

Process description, capacity, shift pattern, equipment, layout, production yield, quality control, maintenance, technology choice, bottlenecks, make/buy decisions and operating workflow.

Evidence: machinery quotations, process data, layout, technical specifications, capacity calculations.

Location, site & infrastructure

Site ownership/lease, access, zoning/use, built-up area, utilities, water, power, transport, waste handling, connectivity and site readiness. Flag approvals and dependencies.

Evidence: site documents, utility availability, maps/photos, local approvals and quotations.

Inputs, suppliers & manpower

Raw materials, packaging, consumables, suppliers, price/availability, inventory policy, logistics, manpower by role/shift, wages, training and key-person dependencies.

Evidence: supplier quotations, lead times, wage basis, input-output norms.

Implementation & readiness

Milestones from sanction to commissioning: land/site, approvals, civil works, machinery order, installation, recruitment, trial runs and commercial production. Identify critical path and long-lead items.

Evidence: dated milestone plan, quotations, approval status, responsible owner.

Project cost & means of finance

Land/building, plant and machinery, installation, preliminary/pre-operative expenses, contingency, margin for working capital and funding sources. Explain estimates and promoter contribution evidence.

Evidence: quotations, estimates, invoices, valuation and funding confirmation.

Financial viability & repayment

Projected P&L, balance sheet, cash flow, working capital, break-even, DSCR, debt schedule, repayment source and key assumptions. Explain the drivers; do not repeat every statement line in prose.

Evidence: deterministic engine output, linked assumptions, debt terms.

Risks, sensitivities & mitigations

Market, input, execution, technology, regulatory, environmental, people, customer concentration and financial risks. Include downside scenarios, trigger indicators, mitigation owners and residual exposure.

Evidence: risk register linked to assumptions and scenario engine.

Conclusion, conditions & declarations

Neutral summary of the case, unresolved information, conditions before implementation, promoter declaration, basis/limitations and preparation disclaimer. Do not issue a fake credit sanction recommendation.

Evidence: completion checklist, user attestations, open-items register.

Annexes: the supporting record

A · Financial schedules

Detailed P&L, balance sheet, cash flow, DSCR, repayment schedule, working capital assessment, depreciation, tax assumptions, sensitivity workings.

B · Technical schedules

Equipment list, specifications, capacity calculations, raw-material norms, manpower, utility loads, process flow, layout, civil estimate and implementation chart.

C · Evidence pack

Promoter documents, site records, quotations, supplier evidence, market sources, customer letters/orders, approvals and photographs—with dates and labels.

05 · Visual and editorial system

Every chart must earn its place.

The report should not be a chart gallery. Charts are for patterns, comparisons, composition and relationships. Tables are for exact values and auditability. Prose is for reasoning, context and implications.

Weak pattern

Chart + table + paragraph

Chart plots annual sales. Table lists the same annual sales. Paragraph says sales rise every year. Three objects, one fact, little additional insight.

Better pattern

Claim + evidence + implication

“The first-year sales plan requires 42% capacity utilisation.” A driver table shows volume, price and ramp-up. A short note explains the customer pipeline and what must be secured before full utilisation.

Mandatory exhibit design contract

  1. Question: What decision or uncertainty does this exhibit address?
  2. Finding: A one-sentence, evidence-backed title—not a generic label.
  3. Evidence: Source, date, unit, period, definition and whether actual, quoted, estimated or assumed.
  4. Interpretation: What does it mean for project design, operations, funding or risk?
  5. Non-duplication: Does another table, chart or paragraph already do this job?
  6. Accessibility: Labels, contrast, grayscale legibility, no colour-only meaning, readable at A4 print size.
Editorial rule: One exhibit should make one point. The title states the finding; the visual/table proves it; the adjacent prose explains why it matters. Do not narrate the axes back to the reader.

Specific treatment of the current specimen

  • Page 1: remove the chart. Use a restrained cover.
  • Page 2: reduce to a decision brief. Move detailed five-year values to the financial chapter/annex.
  • Page 5: retain the process diagram only if it explains the actual production/service system. Replace empty photo frames with optional evidence images in an annex or site section.
  • Page 6: sources-and-uses table should be primary. Keep one funding composition visual only if it improves comprehension.
  • Page 7: use the sales build-up as a driver table. Keep a chart only if it shows product mix, ramp-up or margin trend not obvious from the table.
  • Page 8: repayment page should answer “what cash pays the debt?” and show the lowest coverage year, not merely display a DSCR bar.
  • Page 9: combine sensitivities into a scenario matrix; show assumptions and resulting cash/DSCR together.
  • Pages 11–13: keep full statements in annexes, with consistent units, sign conventions and cross-references.
  • Page 14: replace generic notes with an assumption and evidence register; key evidence should also appear beside the relevant claim.
06 · The missing meat

Build an evidence engine, not a prose generator.

The write-up step should not ask the AI to make a thin report sound impressive. It should help the borrower supply, structure and verify the information that a lender would otherwise have to chase.

Market evidence

  • Target customer groups and buying criteria
  • Geography and reachable market—not unsupported national TAM
  • Competitor/substitute table: product, price, capacity/scale where known, strengths and gaps
  • Customer pipeline: enquiry, trial, order, repeat buyer, LOI—clearly distinguished
  • Pricing basis, channel margin, credit period, seasonality and concentration

Technical and operating evidence

  • Installed capacity and practical capacity, shifts and working days
  • Capacity utilisation ramp and bottleneck
  • Process flow, yield, rejection/scrap, quality checks
  • Equipment list, supplier, quotation date, installation and maintenance
  • Raw materials, consumption norms, supplier alternatives, lead times
  • Power, water, fuel, labour and waste requirements

Promoter and execution evidence

  • Relevant experience and track record
  • Ownership, legal form, management roles and key-person dependencies
  • Promoter contribution: source, amount, timing and proof status
  • Site control, access, land/building readiness
  • Approvals: required, obtained, pending, not applicable—with owner and due date

Financial assumption evidence

  • Unit price and volume build-up, not only annual totals
  • Raw material and conversion cost drivers
  • Receivable, inventory and payable days
  • Fixed/variable cost classification and capacity dependence
  • Depreciation, tax, interest, repayment and working-capital basis
  • Quotation validity, escalation and contingency basis

Do not invent market data. If FinSight cannot substantiate a market-size claim, it should say “not independently verified” or use promoter-supplied evidence with a clear label. A candid limitation increases credibility; fabricated precision destroys it.
07 · Calculation, AI and provenance

Keep the maths deterministic. Make the narrative traceable.

The proposal’s “figure slot” idea is directionally right, but the control system should be more rigorous than scanning for digits.

Source factsUser input / document / source
Typed assumptionsValue, unit, period, evidence class
Calculation engineVersioned formulas and outputs
Narrative bindingApproved metric IDs and citations
Report rendererSame data for HTML and PDF

Required architecture

  1. Canonical project model: one typed schema for project facts, assumptions, source records, financial outputs, evidence status and user attestations.
  2. Metric registry: every calculated figure has a stable ID, formula/version, unit, currency, period, rounding rule and source lineage.
  3. AI narrative contract: the model can summarise, explain and place approved metric references. It cannot calculate, silently alter assumptions or claim that an unverified input is independently verified.
  4. Structured citations: each material claim points to one or more source IDs or is labelled as promoter assertion/model assumption. “AI generated” is not evidence.
  5. Consistency validation: compare every displayed value across summary, charts, tables and annexes against the same canonical metric. Validate signs, units, dates, periods, totals and rounding.
  6. Human review: promoter confirms factual narrative and evidence. FinSight displays unresolved fields and low-confidence assumptions instead of hiding them.
  7. Versioning: report version records model version, assumption set, generation timestamp and source snapshot. Regeneration after a changed assumption must update all dependent sections.

Why digit scanning is insufficient

Failure modeExampleBetter control
Numbers written as words“twenty per cent”, “one lakh”AI may only refer to approved metric tokens for model-derived numerical claims; validate semantic claims against source fields.
Wrong unit or period₹ lakh shown as ₹ crore; FY 2028 shown as calendar 2028Typed units and period metadata; schema-level validation.
Correct value, wrong contextPeak-year DSCR described as minimum DSCRMetric IDs encode meaning, not just value; test narrative-to-metric mapping.

Important product truth: deterministic arithmetic does not make the whole report “verified”. The output is only as reliable as its inputs, assumptions, source quality and model logic. Use labels such as “calculated from stated assumptions”, “promoter-provided”, “supported by quotation” and “independently sourced” precisely.

09 · Definition of world-class

Acceptance tests for the next version.

Do not sign off because the PDF looks polished. Sign off when a reader can understand, challenge and verify the project efficiently.

Reader comprehension

  • A banker can explain the project, funding ask and repayment source after reading the first two pages.
  • Each main chapter answers a distinct question.
  • No paragraph merely repeats a chart or table.
  • Critical risks and missing evidence are easy to find.

Evidence quality

  • Every material market/technical/cost claim has a source or explicit assumption label.
  • Dates, units and evidence classes are visible.
  • Promoter claims are not presented as independent verification.
  • Open conditions have owners and due dates where appropriate.

Financial integrity

  • All displayed financial values reconcile to the canonical engine.
  • Scenario results recompute the whole affected model, not isolated chart values.
  • Units, signs, rounding and financial years are consistent.
  • Assumptions can be changed and the report regenerated without manual patching.

Document craft

  • Charts remain readable in A4 print and grayscale.
  • Tables have repeat headers and do not split awkwardly.
  • Every page has a clear hierarchy and purposeful whitespace.
  • Annexes are navigable; document version and basis are clear.

A practical reviewer test

Give the report to a credit professional who did not build the model. Ask them to find, without assistance: (1) what is being established; (2) who will run it; (3) who will buy; (4) how much can be produced/sold; (5) what evidence supports the sales plan; (6) how much money is required and where it goes; (7) what cash pays the instalments; (8) what can go wrong; (9) what remains unverified. Record time-to-answer and unanswered questions. That is a better quality metric than page count or visual polish.

My final recommendation: keep the financial engine and rebuild the report around a project-evidence model. The report should tell a single coherent story: promoter → opportunity → market → operating design → implementation → investment cost → cash generation → repayment → risks and conditions. Charts are supporting instruments, not the structure of the story.
